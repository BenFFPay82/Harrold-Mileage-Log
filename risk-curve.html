The idea in one screen

A dwelling fire is not a single risk. It is a spectrum of outcomes — from a pan fire that's out on arrival, to a fully developed fire that kills two people. Each outcome has its own likelihood and its own consequence. Plotting them together draws a risk curve: many low-consequence / high-frequency events at one end, rare high-consequence events at the other. We score consequence across four lenses — People, Place, Environment, Economy — using a matrix that deliberately weights consequence above likelihood, then combine them into a single comparable score.

This is a working model with illustrative data. Every consequence and likelihood band below is editable — plug in real CRA-database figures and the matrix, curve and combined scores all recalculate live. Likelihood descriptors and the 7-level definitions are placeholders flagged for the group to agree.

Why this matrix leans on consequence

Cells are numbered along diagonals (the IRM method) so every cell has a unique score and no two outcomes tie. Crucially, within each diagonal the higher-consequence cell takes the higher number — so a rare-but-catastrophic event (low likelihood × high impact) outscores a frequent-but-trivial one (high likelihood × low impact). That is the behaviour we want for life risk.

How this differs from Ian's 2022 method (ACRL)

Ian's Assessment of Community Risk Level ran three quantified assessments, each a simple 5×5 multiplication on a 1–5 scale (5 = highest frequency / most severe):

  • Life risk = incident-frequency score × casualty-rate score
  • Resource / normal-demand risk = incident-frequency score × FRS-resources score
  • Reasonable worst-case scenario = likelihood × consequence (human welfare, FRS response, economic, environmental, community impact)

Two things change in the new approach. First, multiplication weights likelihood and consequence equally; the new matrix deliberately leans on consequence. Second — and the bigger shift — Ian scored each incident type as a single point using average casualty rates, so a dwelling fire became one dot. Tick "Show Ian's single-point method" above to drop that dot onto the curve: it lands at high frequency / low average consequence (green), because averaging buries the rare fatal fires. The curve keeps that fatal tail visible. Ian's frequency was data-led from 5 years of IRS records — exactly the basis we'll use to derive the proportion-of-dwelling-fires likelihood here.
